# Writing Style Guide

Use this guide when drafting the full proposal. The goal is a persuasive, academically grounded, and readable document suitable for Japanese graduate-school application contexts.

## Core Style Principles

### 1. Write in connected prose
The proposal should read like a coherent argument, not a checklist. Use full paragraphs to explain the problem, review prior work, justify the method, and articulate the contribution.

Avoid excessive bullet points except in narrow places where compact structure is genuinely helpful, such as the timeline.

### 2. Be specific, not inflated
Use concrete claims supported by the professor's recent work, the literature review, and the applicant's real background. Do not use vague praise, grandiose predictions, or unsupported novelty claims.

### 3. Keep the argument forward-moving
Each section should naturally answer the implicit question raised by the previous one:
- Introduction: why this problem matters
- Literature review: what is already known
- Gap/questions: what remains unresolved
- Methodology: how the study will address it
- Significance: why the result will matter

### 4. Match language to discipline
- In STEM, prioritize precision, methodological clarity, and operational definitions.
- In Humanities, prioritize conceptual framing, interpretive logic, and source positioning.
- In Social Sciences, prioritize causal caution, variable clarity, and evidence limits.

## Proposal Voice

Aim for a voice that is:
- academically serious
- confident but not overstated
- analytical rather than promotional
- aligned with graduate-level research planning

Good pattern:

> Recent studies have improved X by introducing Y; however, they still leave unresolved the question of Z, especially in contexts where A and B interact.

Avoid patterns such as:
- "This research will completely revolutionize..."
- "I am extremely passionate about..." without analytical substance
- generic statements that could fit any topic

## Literature Integration

Do not stack citations mechanically. Instead, synthesize them.

Prefer:

> Prior research on multimodal diagnosis has largely focused on performance gains from larger models, while more recent studies have shifted attention to robustness under limited supervision. This shift is directly relevant to the proposed study because...

Over:

> Paper A did X. Paper B did Y. Paper C did Z.

Use literature to support one of four functions:
- establish background
- define the current frontier
- expose a gap or limitation
- justify a methodological choice

## Applicant-Fit Integration

The proposal should show why the project is feasible for **this applicant** in **this professor's environment**. Integrate the applicant's background where it matters most:
- prior projects that prepare the applicant for the methods
- technical skills relevant to data, experiments, or analysis
- domain familiarity that improves feasibility

Do not turn the proposal into a personal statement. Mention the applicant only when it strengthens feasibility or fit.

## Figure Suggestions

Insert 3-5 figure suggestions where they would genuinely help understanding. Use exactly this format:

`> **[Figure X Suggestion]** *Title: ...* Content: ...`

Good figure candidates include:
- conceptual framework diagrams
- method pipelines
- dataset or source schema
- timeline visualizations
- comparison frameworks in literature review

## Section-Level Guidance

### Abstract
Write the abstract after the outline is stable. It should be compact, precise, and self-contained.

### Introduction
Move from broader context to the concrete unresolved problem. End with the proposal objective.

### Literature Review
Organize by themes, approaches, or debates. Use transitions to show comparison and evolution.

### Methodology
Explain not just **what** will be done, but **why** that method is appropriate and feasible.

### Timeline
Keep it realistic. Avoid overloading the first term with too many deliverables.

### Significance
Differentiate between expected scholarly contribution and possible practical value.

## Language Consistency

If the user requests English, write the full proposal in English. If the user requests Chinese, write the full proposal in Chinese. Keep paper titles in their original language in references.

Do not mix languages casually inside analytical paragraphs.

## Citation Discipline

- Never invent references.
- Prefer recent and directly relevant references.
- Keep citation style consistent within the same draft.
- If some details are uncertain, verify before citing or mark them as pending verification during drafting.

## Final Standard

A strong final draft should feel like a serious proposal that could plausibly be submitted after user review and factual polishing. It should demonstrate topic fit, literature grounding, methodological feasibility, and clear contribution without sounding generic or fabricated.
