# Structure Guide

Use this guide when building the proposal outline. Adapt the exact section depth to the user's target program, but preserve the overall logic of **problem → gap → question → method → contribution**.

## Standard Proposal Skeleton

### 1. Title
Write a precise, researchable title. Prefer a structure such as:

`Method or perspective + target problem/domain + expected outcome`

Avoid vague titles such as "Research on AI" or "Study of Education Reform".

### 2. Abstract
Write a compact abstract that covers five elements in one connected narrative:
1. background context
2. unresolved problem
3. proposed objective or hypothesis
4. methodology
5. expected contribution

For a ~3,000-word proposal, the abstract is usually 150-250 words.

### 3. Introduction
The introduction should establish why the topic matters now. Move from broad field context to the specific problem the proposal addresses. End the section by stating the core objective of the proposed study.

The introduction should answer:
- What broader field or social/technical context does this proposal sit in?
- Why is the problem important?
- Why is it still unresolved?
- Why is this professor/lab an appropriate fit?

### 4. Literature Review
Organize literature thematically instead of summarizing papers one by one. A strong literature review usually contains three layers:
- major streams of prior work
- what recent work has improved
- what remains missing or contradictory

Use literature to justify the research gap, not to display reading volume alone.

### 5. Research Gap and Questions
This section is the hinge between literature review and methodology.

State clearly:
- what gap remains
- why the gap matters
- the main research question
- 2-4 sub-questions or objectives if needed

Research questions should be specific, answerable, and method-linked.

### 6. Methodology
Explain how the study will answer the research question. Include only methods that are realistic for the applicant's actual background and the target lab's likely resources.

Depending on the field, this section may include:
- data or corpus sources
- experiment or study design
- model, framework, or analytical method
- evaluation metrics
- validation strategy
- ethical considerations where relevant

Do not list methods mechanically. Explain why each method is suitable.

### 7. Timeline
Provide a staged plan. For Japanese graduate applications, a simple semester-based or quarter-based plan is usually sufficient.

A practical timeline often includes:
- literature consolidation
- data collection or environment setup
- pilot study or baseline experiment
- main analysis or system development
- writing and revision

### 8. Expected Contributions and Significance
Separate **academic significance** from **practical significance** when possible.

Typical contribution categories:
- theoretical clarification
- methodological improvement
- empirical evidence in a new setting
- dataset, benchmark, tool, or workflow contribution
- policy, educational, or industrial relevance

### 9. References
Use a consistent citation style throughout the proposal. Prefer recent, credible, and directly relevant sources. For PhD-level drafts, 40 or more references is a good default target unless the user specifies otherwise.

## Adjustment by Degree Type

| Degree target | Typical framing | Scope expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Master's | Focus on feasibility, training value, and a narrower question | One tractable problem with a realistic method |
| PhD | Focus on originality, deeper gap articulation, and stronger literature grounding | Broader program with multiple stages or linked studies |

## Adjustment by Discipline

| Discipline | Method section emphasis | Contribution emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| STEM | experiment design, datasets, models, benchmarks, reproducibility | performance, mechanism, system value |
| Humanities | primary texts/materials, interpretive lens, historiography | reinterpretation, conceptual contribution |
| Social Sciences | sampling, variables, fieldwork/surveys/interviews, causal limits | explanation, evidence quality, policy relevance |

## Outline Quality Standard

A usable outline is not just a list of headings. It should already show:
- the exact problem to be solved
- the logic connecting each section
- the likely evidence base
- the feasibility path linked to the applicant and professor
